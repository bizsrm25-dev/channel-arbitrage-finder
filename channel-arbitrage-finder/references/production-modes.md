# Production modes

**Additions A4–A6.** Victor defines quality by audience preference at 02:56–03:12. His team improved the questions and pacing in one basketball format. In another case, he used the team itself to make an engineering comparison visible. Choose a production mode that preserves the specific reason a viewer stays for the result. The workflows and time allowances here are proposed production plans. See [Method Brief, §§3, 7 and 9](method.md).

## Choose a feasible version

Write the payoff as an observable action, such as “the viewer predicts which repair lasts, then watches both undergo the same test.” Name the evidence the payoff needs on screen. Rule out any candidate whose core proof requires a face, a real demonstration, specialist gear or technical skill the user cannot supply. If a new version preserves the payoff, give it a new format description and check whether research supports that version. A generated image of an experiment cannot establish its result.

Build a costed first episode before ranking production fit. The following are **sample estimates** for one solo 45–90 second episode using familiar tools. They include the upload and exclude a first-time setup, which should be estimated separately. For long-form, count actual scenes, interviews, rights checks, demonstrations and revisions. Build the estimate from those tasks. Use the first timed episode to revise the remaining 30-day schedule.

| Mode and example | Research and write | Capture or produce assets | Edit, check and publish | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Faceless + AI · three illustrated scenes | 45 min | 60 min | 45 min | 2 h 30 min |
| Faceless, human-made · one screen demonstration | 30 min | 30 min | 45 min | 1 h 45 min |
| On-camera · one host and one demonstration | 30 min | 45 min | 45 min | 2 h |
| Hybrid · host introduction, two diagrams and a filmed result | 40 min | 65 min | 55 min | 2 h 40 min |

## Faceless + AI

1. Start with a sourced script and a visual job for every beat. Decide which details require a real diagram, actual photograph or measured value. Use generated illustration for a reconstruction or analogy, and label it where the viewer could reasonably mistake it for evidence.
2. Choose one [visual style](art-styles.md), a small palette with actual colour values, a frame shape and a continuity rule for the main object. Write **three complete generation prompts** for the first episode, one each for the question, the mechanism and the reveal. Each prompt specifies subject, action, camera, lighting, palette, duration if moving, and space for captions. Keep factual labels and numbers in the edit.
3. Pick the source of narration. Give a licensed synthetic voice an original delivery brief such as “curious, low-key English narration, pause after the question, stress the answer once.” Check pronunciation, unit names and audio rights. Offer the user's own voice if they chose that mix.
4. Generate the minimum useful set of stills or clips. Inspect continuity, anatomy, machinery and dates. Edit the chosen assets to the narration, add explanatory labels by hand and watch once with sound off. Write down where human reporting, testing or interpretation enters this episode.
5. List the chosen generator, voice and editor, the plan actually available to the user, commercial-use conditions, and the estimated credits for accepted and rejected takes. Existing tools plus stills can be easier to cost than AI video. Recheck the vendor’s current billing interval and credit rules. [Runway credit documentation](https://help.runwayml.com/hc/en-us/articles/15124877443219-How-do-credits-work)

## Faceless, human-made

1. Choose a physical or digital action the creator can perform. Capture the creator's own hands, device or screen. For archival context, record the item URL, creator, rights statement, required credit and exact excerpt. Check permissions for the specific asset. [Library of Congress free-to-use collections](https://www.loc.gov/free-to-use/)
2. For screen work, prepare a clean demo account, enlarge the interface to phone-readable size and record the initial state, one action and its result. For hands-only work, fix a phone overhead, mark the workspace and record a wide shot plus one close-up where the result changes. For animation, draw a few reusable layers and animate the part responsible for the change.
3. Record the creator's own narration in a quiet space. Read a short paragraph at a time, leave room for visual evidence and check the cut on headphones. Match each spoken instruction to the moment it can be seen. Keep authentic pauses where the viewer needs time to inspect the result.
4. Specify the edit rhythm, source list, sound treatment and what the thumbnail can show from the footage. A workable no-licence-fee software stack on compatible hardware is OBS for capture, Audacity for narration and Shotcut for editing. [OBS Studio](https://obsproject.com/) · [Audacity](https://www.audacityteam.org/) · [Shotcut](https://www.shotcut.org/)

## On-camera

1. Match the shot list to the gear in the profile. With a phone, mark the tripod or stable support, lock focus and exposure when available, and record a short audio check at the actual speaking distance. Use a medium shot at eye height for a direct explanation and a wider view that keeps the whole action inside the frame for a demonstration.
2. Place the existing window or key light to one side of the presenter. Rehearse the action at its limits to check shadows, glare and cropping. Set the microphone near the speaker and listen for clothing noise or echo. Mark positions for the camera, subject and props. Note the working settings so a later episode matches.
3. Film the question, complete demonstration and result as separate takes. A confident unscripted speaker can use three cue cards. For a scripted host, break the text into short statements and look into the lens for the question and conclusion. Speak while showing the relevant object, then pause for the viewer to inspect it.
4. Specify three cutaway shots, including a tight view of the crucial change, a side angle that shows the whole movement, and the final state held long enough to compare. Confirm that any featured participant agreed to the planned use. Reset the set and log the best takes before packing up.

## Hybrid

1. Assign a mode and purpose to every segment. In a 60-second episode, the host asks which setup works in the first eight seconds. At 8–20s, screen capture establishes the settings. At 20–45s, real footage shows the test. At 45–54s, an original overlay explains the mechanism. At 54–60s, the host gives the result and invites the next test.
2. List who supplies the voice, footage and graphics in each segment. Preserve the creator’s stated face and AI boundaries. Use animation for an invisible mechanism and authentic footage for an observed result.
3. Storyboard handoffs with a recurring object, consistent framing or a continuous narration track. Record the on-camera material in one session, collect supporting images, and assemble a rough cut before polishing transitions. Include the time for both capture systems in the plan.

## Costs, capacity and launch checks

- Quote verified plan prices in the user's currency or show the original currency and date. State whether the rate is monthly, annual, per credit or per seat. Include new cash outlay, subscription commitments, asset licences, freelance quotes and one-time purchases in separate rows. Mark an unavailable quote **unverified**. A spending cap is a budget, not a vendor price.
- Compute monthly cash as fixed subscriptions actually needed + new gear purchases for that month + variable per-video spend × planned uploads. Full production cost per episode equals attributable monthly fixed costs divided by planned uploads, plus variable costs. List unpaid labour in hours and add a cash value only when the user supplies a rate. Avoid adding the price of included credits twice.
- **Illustrative budget arithmetic.** Four uploads using a hypothetical 20-unit monthly plan plus 2 units in paid assets each would cost 28 units that month or 7 per upload. With existing gear, a free editor and entirely original assets, proposed additional cash can be zero. Include existing paid subscriptions separately and identify any limits on reuse.
- Compare weekly labour, including research administration and comment review, with the confirmed hours. If a priced or timed version exceeds either limit, simplify the shot list, reduce cadence with the user's agreement, or drop the candidate. Treat the launch plans for the three alternatives as separate budgets.
- Give each first upload one falsifiable test, such as whether showing the real result earlier holds viewers until the reveal. Compare similar episodes at similar ages. Use elapsed edit time, actual spend and usable takes to decide whether a later batch is affordable. Scale through other creators only after the format repeatedly works, with original briefs, a quality review and a viable cost per finished episode.
- Recheck the applicable platform policy at report time. For YouTube, review whether realistic synthetic scenes require disclosure and whether the actual episodes offer distinct educational or creative value. Preserve source records, releases and the creator's contribution.
